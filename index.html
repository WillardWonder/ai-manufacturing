01

The basics

What AI actually is.

"AI" gets used for four very different things. Knowing which one someone means is half the battle when a vendor comes calling.

Reads and writes

AI assistants

Also called generative AI or large language models. You type or talk to it in plain English, and it writes, summarizes, translates, analyzes, and answers back.

On the shop floorTurning a veteran operator's explanation into a written work instruction.

Learns from your data

Predictive AI

Also called machine learning. It finds patterns in sensor, production, or sales data and uses them to forecast what comes next.

On the shop floorFlagging a spindle bearing that's starting to fail, before it fails.

Sees

Computer vision

Cameras paired with software trained to recognize what they're looking at.

On the shop floorSpotting surface defects, counting parts, reading labels, or checking for missing components at line speed.

Does

Automation and robotics

Robots, cobots, and software bots that follow rules. Many aren't AI at all. AI is what lets some of them handle variation instead of exact repetition.

On the shop floorA cobot that adjusts its pick when parts arrive slightly out of position.

Most of what you can use tomorrow, with no new equipment and no integration project, is the first kind: AI assistants. That's where the rest of this overview spends most of its time.

How an AI assistant works, in one minute.

  • 1

    It predicts. It doesn't know.

    It writes the most likely next words based on patterns from an enormous amount of text. Most likely isn't the same as true, and it isn't the same as your shop's way of doing things.

    SoIt can sound confident and still be wrong. Check anything that matters.
  • 2

    It fills in what you leave blank.

    Ask a room of people to have it draw "a bowl of fruit" and you get a room full of nearly identical, safe, generic bowls. Every decision you skip, it makes for you.

    SoVague requests get generic answers. Specific requests get useful ones.
  • 3

    It only knows what you give it.

    It doesn't know your parts, your customers, your machines, or your procedures unless you tell it or attach them.

    SoThe quality of what you give it decides the quality of what you get.

Remember

  • "AI" means four different things. Ask which one.
  • AI assistants predict. They don't know your shop until you tell them.
02

The landscape

The tools, from easiest to biggest lift.

You don't need to buy anything to start. Most shops should begin at the top of this list and only move down when a real need shows up.

  • Start here

    General AI assistants

    ChatGPTMicrosoft CopilotGoogle GeminiClaude

    Chat-based assistants that write, summarize, translate, and analyze documents and spreadsheets you attach. Free versions exist; paid business plans add stronger data protections and admin controls.

    Cost and effort: free to low monthly cost per person, nothing to install.

  • Next

    AI inside software you already own

    Microsoft 365 CopilotERP and MES add-onsMaintenance software features

    AI features built into Word, Excel, Outlook, and Teams, and increasingly into ERP, MES, quality, and maintenance systems. It works inside your own files and records.

    Cost and effort: usually an add-on license. Ask your vendors what's included, what's coming, and what happens to your data.

  • Custom

    Assistants and small tools built for your shop

    Custom GPTsCopilot agentsSmall AI-built tools

    An assistant loaded with your own SOPs, product specs, or style, so anyone can ask it questions. Or a simple calculator, tracker, or checklist that AI helps you build in an afternoon.

    Cost and effort: low cost, some setup time, and someone who owns keeping it accurate.

  • Biggest lift

    Industrial AI systems

    Vision inspectionPredictive maintenanceDemand forecastingScheduling optimization

    Purpose-built systems with cameras, sensors, and data connections to your equipment and records. Powerful when the problem is clear and the data is good.

    Cost and effort: a real project with a budget, integration, clean data, and a pilot. Start with a specific problem, not with the technology.

Product names and features change fast. These are examples as of late 2026, not endorsements.

Remember

  • Start with a general assistant and a real task.
  • Buy bigger systems to solve specific problems, not to "do AI."
03

On and off the floor

What it can do in your shop.

The best early wins are tasks that are repeated, written, and eat hours: the paperwork around the work.

Production

  • Work instructions and SOPs from an expert's explanation
  • Shift handoff summaries from notes
  • Changeover and startup checklists

Quality

  • First drafts of 8D and corrective action reports
  • Root-cause brainstorming with 5 Whys or fishbone
  • Spotting trends in a nonconformance spreadsheet

Maintenance

  • Troubleshooting guides from equipment manuals
  • Preventive maintenance checklists
  • Summaries of work order history for a machine

Sales and front office

  • First drafts of quote responses and RFQ replies
  • Summarizing long customer specs and emails
  • Product descriptions and capability sheets

Purchasing and supply chain

  • Side-by-side supplier comparisons
  • Plain-language summaries of contract terms
  • Lead time and price analysis from a spreadsheet

Safety, HR, and training

  • Toolbox talks and safety reminders
  • Job descriptions and interview questions
  • Training quizzes and translated instructions, checked by a fluent speaker

The work stays yours. The blank page doesn't have to be.

Remember

  • Look for tasks that are repeated, written, and time-consuming.
  • AI drafts. People decide and sign off.
04

A worked example

Turn what one person knows into something everyone can use.

Every shop has someone like Dale: 31 years on the same press, and the only one who really knows the die change. Here's how AI helps get that knowledge out of his head and onto the floor.

Start with the expert, not the keyboard.

Dale talked through his die change while someone recorded on a phone. Most phones transcribe a voice memo for free. This is what he said, word for word.

Dale's voice memo, transcribed

"Okay so first thing, you lock it out, obviously, main disconnect and the air. Then I always run the slide down to bottom dead center before I pull anything. Some guys don't and that's how you get pinched. Pull the old die, use the cart, not the forklift, the forklift guys always ding the shoes. Clean the bolster, I mean really clean it, one chip under there and your parts are out. Set the new die, line up the locating pins, then the clamps. Snug them, then torque them, there's a number on the sheet in the office. Shut height, you check that before you cycle, every time. Then inch it through once, then one part, then you call quality for first article before you run. Oh, and the scrap chute, swap it if you're going from the four-inch brackets to the long ones."

This is the hard part, and it isn't AI.The knowledge has to come from a person. AI's job is to turn it into something usable.

Ask vaguely, get something generic.

Write an SOP for a die change.

What came back (excerpt)

1. Purpose: This procedure ensures safe and efficient die changes in accordance with company standards. 2. Scope: Applies to all stamping presses. 3. Responsibilities: The Production Manager shall ensure all personnel are trained... 4. Procedure: Ensure proper PPE is worn. Remove the die using appropriate lifting equipment...

It filled in every blank.Nothing here is exactly wrong, and none of it is Dale's. It says "appropriate lifting equipment" when Dale said use the cart and never the forklift. It doesn't know your press, your people, or your rules.

Tell it who it's for, what it should look like, and what it may not do.

Turn the transcript below into a one-page work instruction for the Press 4 die change. Who it's for: operators with less than a year of experience. Some read English as a second language. Format: numbered steps, one action per step, short sentences. Safety steps first, marked SAFETY. Rules: Use only what's in the transcript. Don't add steps he didn't say. If anything is missing or unclear, list it under "Questions for the supervisor" instead of guessing. Transcript: [Dale's voice memo]

Three things changed the result.The audience, the format, and a rule against making things up. None of it is fancy wording. It's context only you have.

A draft that's actually Dale's.

What came back

PRESS 4 DIE CHANGE: WORK INSTRUCTION SAFETY 1. Lock out the main disconnect and the air supply. SAFETY 2. Run the slide down to bottom dead center before removing anything. 3. Remove the old die with the die cart. Do not use the forklift. 4. Clean the bolster completely. One chip can put parts out of spec. 5. Set the new die. Line up the locating pins. 6. Snug the clamps. Then torque them to the value on the setup sheet. 7. Check shut height before cycling the press. 8. Inch the press through one full stroke. 9. Run one part. Call Quality for first-article inspection before running production. 10. If changing from 4-inch brackets to long brackets, swap the scrap chute. Questions for the supervisor - What is the clamp torque value, and where is the setup sheet kept? - What is the correct shut height for each die? - Does lockout include any other energy sources, such as hydraulic or stored pressure?

Every step traces back to Dale.And the gaps it couldn't fill became questions instead of guesses. That last question about other energy sources is one a supervisor needs to answer.

Build on it in the same conversation.

Keep the steps exactly as written. Add a Spanish version below it.
Now write a five-question check a supervisor can use to confirm a new operator understands this.

One conversation, three useful pieces.The work instruction, a translation, and a training check. A fluent speaker still reviews the translation, especially the safety steps.

A person checks it, finishes it, and signs it.

Dale and his supervisor answer the three questions, add the torque value and shut heights, have the Spanish version checked, then sign, date, and post it at the press.

  • Every step matches how the job is really done
  • Every number comes from the setup sheet, not the AI
  • Safety steps reviewed against your lockout program
  • Signed and dated by the people responsible

The time moves from writing to reviewing.The knowledge stays Dale's. The decisions and the sign-off stay human. This works for any job that lives in one person's head.

Try it at your shop.

Record someone explaining a job they know cold, transcribe it, and use this prompt. Remove anything confidential first.

Turn the transcript below into a one-page work instruction for [the job]. Who it's for: [who will use it and how experienced they are]. Format: numbered steps, one action per step, short sentences. Safety steps first, marked SAFETY. Rules: Use only what's in the transcript. Don't add steps that weren't said. If anything is missing or unclear, list it under "Questions for the supervisor" instead of guessing. Transcript: [paste it here]

Remember

  • The knowledge comes from your people.
  • Context turns a generic answer into your answer.
  • A person always reviews and signs off.
05

How to get good results

You don't need magic words.

Prompting isn't about secret phrases. Four habits do almost all the work, and you just saw every one of them in Dale's example.

01

Know what finished looks like.

Before you type anything, say who will use the result and what they'll be able to do with it. "A new operator can run the die change from one page."

02

Give it the context only you have.

Who it's for, the format, your source material, your rules, and what it must not do. Specific beats long.

03

Steer it like a conversation.

Rarely right on the first try, and that's normal. "Keep this, change that." "What are you assuming?" "Ask me what you need to know."

04

Watch where it's taking you.

A GPS reroutes but keeps your destination. AI can quietly change the destination and make it sound reasonable. Check it against what you set out to do.

AI can help find the route. You own the destination.

06

Guardrails

Use it without getting burned.

Most AI problems in manufacturing come from what goes in and from what nobody checked on the way out.

Don't paste in

  • Customer drawings, specs, or pricing under NDA
  • Export-controlled or defense-related technical data
  • Employee personal or medical information
  • Passwords, account numbers, or system access details
  • Anything your company or customer contracts say stays inside

Always check

  • Every number: dimensions, torque values, tolerances, quantities
  • Every safety step, against your actual programs
  • Names, dates, part numbers, and sources it cites
  • Translations, with a fluent speaker
  • That it's still answering the question you asked
Free versus business accounts. Free consumer accounts may use your conversations to improve their models unless you change the settings. Business plans generally offer stronger data protections. Read the terms, check the settings, and when in doubt, describe the situation instead of pasting the real document.

Put a simple rule in writing.

You don't need a 30-page policy to start. One page that says which tools are approved, what data never goes in, and that a person reviews anything that leaves the building covers most of the risk.

Remember

  • If you wouldn't email it to a stranger, don't paste it in.
  • Check every number and every safety step.
07

Monday morning

Where to start.

Skip the big strategy for now. Prove it on one small, real task, then grow from what works.

  1. Pick one annoying task.

    Repeated, written, and time-consuming. A weekly report, a type of customer email, a missing work instruction.

  2. Write down what finished looks like.

    Who uses the result, and what they'll be able to do with it.

  3. Try it with non-sensitive material.

    Use a general assistant and the four habits. Expect a few rounds of back and forth.

  4. Compare it to the old way.

    Was it faster? Was it as good once a person reviewed it? Be honest about both.

  5. Share what worked, and write the rule.

    Save the prompt that worked, show a coworker, and put your one-page guardrails in writing before it spreads.

Now picture one for your shop.

A scrap and rework calculator. A changeover timer. A quote estimator. A training quiz for new hires. A checklist that prints itself. The same approach works: describe the problem, what goes in, what should come out, and who uses it.

Build a single-page web tool that works offline for this problem: [describe the problem]. The people using it are [who]. They'll enter [inputs] and need to see [outputs]. Include a print button and a short note reminding users to check the results. Ask me up to five questions before you start building.
A prototype isn't a finished system. Before a shop depends on a tool like this, someone has to own it: test it, decide where the data lives, keep it accurate, and decide who's allowed to change it.

Remember

  • If you can describe the problem, AI can help you build a first version.
  • Someone still owns, tests, and maintains it.

09

Prompts to keep.

Starting points for common manufacturing tasks. Fill in the brackets, remove anything confidential, and expect to steer from there. They work in ChatGPT, Copilot, or any AI assistant.

Toolbox talk

Safety and training

Write a five-minute toolbox talk on [topic] for [type of crew] at a [type of manufacturing] shop. Plain language, one real-world example, and three questions to ask the crew at the end. Don't invent statistics.

Customer email reply

Sales and front office

Draft a reply to the customer email below. Our goal: [what you want to happen]. Tone: professional and direct. Don't promise dates, prices, or anything not stated in my notes. My notes: [key facts] Customer email: [paste it, with confidential details removed]

Corrective action first draft

Quality

Help me draft an 8D corrective action report for this problem: [describe the defect, where it was found, and how many parts]. Walk me through 5 Whys one question at a time before you write anything. Mark anything you're assuming.

Troubleshooting guide

Maintenance

Using only the manual section I'll paste below, create a one-page troubleshooting guide for [machine]: symptom, likely cause, what to check, and when to stop and call maintenance. If the manual doesn't cover something, say so. Manual section: [paste it]

Supplier comparison

Purchasing

Compare these supplier quotes in a table: price, lead time, minimum order, payment terms, and anything unusual. Then list the questions I should ask each supplier before deciding. Quotes: [paste the key details]

Meeting to action items

Everyone

Turn these meeting notes into a list of action items with an owner and due date for each. If an owner or date is missing, flag it instead of guessing. Notes: [paste them]

Job description

HR and hiring

Write a job posting for a [role] at a [size and type] manufacturer in [town]. Must-haves: [list]. Nice-to-haves: [list]. Shift and pay: [details]. Plain, honest language. No buzzwords.

When the answer is generic

A follow-up for any task

What assumptions did you make that I didn't tell you? Ask me the questions you need answered to make this specific to our shop.

AI won't replace the people who know how your shop runs. It can make sure what they know gets written down, shared, and used.
